HALO Mars: Hazard Aware Layered Operations on Mars

A real-time navigation tool that updates the route home as geographic, atmospheric, and astronaut's physiological conditions change.

What the map shows
  • Real-time navigation: Geographic terrain, atmospheric conditions, and physiological strain are recalculated together as the crew moves.
  • Point of Safe Return: A live boundary instead of a distance circle. Green is under 75% of reserves, amber is 75–100%, and red means turn back.
  • Lowest-cost way home: A blue route that cannot cross cliffs steeper than 33°, and that costs more on loose sand.
  • Two Mars sites: Mount Sharp (Curiosity) and Jezero Delta (Perseverance).
Why a distance circle is not enough

The walk back can cost far more oxygen and energy than the walk out, once steep climbs, loose sand, sample weight, headwind, and a solar storm are included. Two kilometers downhill on firm ground is not the same trip as two kilometers back uphill with an extra 25 kg of samples.

Slide 02 of 07 • Five NASA map layers

Five NASA map layers, one decision, one view

One layer is on screen at a time. Turning a layer on or off changes how far the crew can safely go.

Cliffs

Steep cliffs · HiRISE / MOLA

Blocks cliffs steeper than 33° when this layer is on, and counts the extra cost of walking uphill.

Loose sand

Loose sand · THEMIS

Loose drift sand costs more energy than firm bedrock.

Sample weight

Sample targets · CRISM

Science targets are marked. The rock samples add to the load only while this layer is on.

Wind

Wind · MEDA / REMS

One wind for the whole site. Walking into it costs more than walking with it.

Radiation

Radiation · RAD + DONKI

Dose is lower where the horizon blocks more sky. A DONKI solar-storm alert raises the dose when the feed is up.

Why each layer matters: These five layers are calibrated map data for those instruments. DONKI updates live when the feed responds. Turning a layer off shows how much safe ground that hazard removes.
Slide 03 of 07 • Walk out and walk back

The walk back is a different cost

The map adds the cost to get there and the cost to get home.

Two scores, then one map
  • Walk back: From every place on the map, score the trip home to base. The climb is often the hard part.
  • Walk out: From where the astronaut is now, score the trip outward. The color of the map is those two costs added together.
  • What runs out first: Oxygen, battery, calories, or radiation. Heart rate, blood oxygen, core temperature, and suit carbon dioxide change how fast those reserves are used.
Waypoint 3 — the samples are too heavy
TURN BACK • Waypoint 3, 2.8 km from base

The demo adds 25 kg of samples, so the crew carries 40 kg of rock and 90 kg with the suit, at 168 BPM. Base is still 2.8 km away, and that scene puts the walk back past the oxygen and calorie reserves.

Slide 04 of 07 • The wrist watch

A watch that still points home

If suit power fails, the crew still needs a bearing back to base.

What the watch shows
  • Direction home: The safe boundary, the bearing back to base, how far is left, and which reserve is running out: effort, oxygen, battery, or radiation.
  • Map off: One tap switches off the 3D map. A simple 1 Hz display keeps pointing home.
  • One coin cell: That display draws about 42 mW, 5+ hours on one CR2032 coin cell (estimated).
Why the map can be switched off

During a solar storm or a suit power fault, a bright 3D map drains a small battery. Switching the map off keeps the direction home for 5+ hours on one coin cell.

Slide 05 of 07 • Zero-GPS match

A horizon drawn from the elevation map

With no GPS on Mars, the astronaut sees a location for the horizon at the pin. The way back is drawn from that pin.

What the horizon does
  • Every cell: 36 directions. Each one records the highest angle of the ground.
  • Location: The horizon at the pin is compared with the local elevation map. The astronaut sees the DEM cell and the 10° turn that agree. The turn comes from the pin’s place on the grid, or from the device compass when it is connected.
  • Way back: The path is drawn from that pin back to base. Where the ground blocks more of the sky, the radiation dose is lower, and that dose is one of the costs that shapes the way back and how far the crew can still go.
Slide 06 of 07 • BYOD: bring your own device and data

Your elevation, a heart-rate reading, and a route to export

Drop in an elevation file, pair a heart-rate sensor, and download the route.

What you can connect
  • Your own elevation file: Drop in an elevation grid. It is fit to the 10 km map, and the safe-return boundary updates. Sand and mineral layers for that file are estimated from the heights.
  • A heart-rate sensor: Pair a standard Bluetooth heart-rate sensor, in a browser that supports it. That reading replaces the heart-rate control, and the boundary is recalculated.
  • Download the route: Save the safe-zone lines and the return path as GeoJSON, and the return path as GPX. A CSV saves the time log.
The app says what is live
  • A check at startup: A self-check runs once on the site loaded at startup and shows pass or fail. The map still runs if a check fails.
  • Live or simulated: Mars Trek imagery and DONKI alerts are marked live when those feeds respond. The Gale and Jezero terrain grids stay a calibrated simulation.
Runs in the browser

HALO Mars runs in the browser. The return path can be saved as GPX, and the safe-zone lines as GeoJSON.
